# The Over Four Decades of Battle Between the Cassini Family and Sir Isaac Newton Over the True Shape of the Earth 

The-Over-Four-Decades-of-Battle-Between-the-Cassini-Family-and-Sir-Isaac-Newton-Over-the-True-Shape-of-the-Earth.md 

The "battle" between Giovanni Domenico Cassini (along with his son Jacques) and Sir Isaac Newton was a fierce 18th-century scientific dispute over the true shape of the Earth.

While both agreed the Earth was not a perfect sphere, they clashed over how it was deformed—a debate that sparked national rivalry between France and England and tested the validity of Newton’s theory of universal gravitation.

## 1. The Competing Theories

### • Newton’s Theory (Oblate Spheroid): 
Based on his laws of gravity and centrifugal force, Newton argued that the Earth’s rotation causes it to bulge at the equator and flatten at the poles, much like a grapefruit or a spinning ball of clay.

### • Cassini’s Theory (Prolate Spheroid): 
Relying on early geodesic land surveys conducted across France (the Paris Meridian), Cassini and his son Jacques concluded the exact opposite. Their measurements suggested a degree of latitude grew shorter as one moved north, meaning the Earth was elongated at the poles and pinched at the equator, like a lemon or an egg.

## 2. Why It Mattered

This was far more than a geometric nitpick; the entire framework of modern physics was on the line:

### • Testing Gravity: 
If the Cassinis were right, Newton’s Principia Mathematica and his inverse-square law of gravity would be proven fundamentally flawed.

### • National Pride: 
The French Academy of Sciences largely backed the Cassinis' empirical "hands-on" measurements, viewing Newton's purely mathematical approach with skepticism. Meanwhile, the British Royal Society fiercely defended Newton.

### • Navigation: 
Knowing the Earth's exact curvature was vital for creating accurate maps and navigating safely across oceans.

## 3. The Expeditions That Settled the Score

Because measurements within France alone were too small to rule out human or instrument error, the French Academy of Sciences organized two historic, grueling expeditions in the 1730s to measure a degree of latitude at extreme locations:

### • The Equator (1735): 
Led by Charles Marie de La Condamine, Pierre Bouguer, and Louis Godin, a team traveled to the Viceroyalty of Peru (modern-day Ecuador) to measure the curvature at the equator.

### • The Arctic Circle (1736): 
Led by Pierre Louis Maupertuis, a team traveled to Lapland (northern Sweden/Finland) to measure the curvature near the North Pole.

## 4. The Verdict

Maupertuis returned from Lapland first in 1737. His measurements proved that a degree of latitude near the pole was noticeably longer than in France—meaning the Earth is indeed flattened at the poles. The Peruvian expedition later confirmed the equatorial bulge.

Newton was vindicated. **Voltaire** famously mocked Maupertuis upon his return for having ***"flattened the poles and the Cassinis,"*** **cementing Newton’s universal law of gravitation as the foundation of physics.**
