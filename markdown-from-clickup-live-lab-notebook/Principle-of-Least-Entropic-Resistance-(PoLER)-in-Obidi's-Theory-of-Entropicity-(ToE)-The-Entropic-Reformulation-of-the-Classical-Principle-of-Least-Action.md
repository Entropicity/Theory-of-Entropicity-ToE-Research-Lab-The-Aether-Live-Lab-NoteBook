The proposal that physical systems change, move, or evolve along paths of least entropic resistance was introduced by John Onimisi Obidi. This concept is formalized as the Principle of Least Entropic Resistance (PoLER), which serves as a core foundational pillar of his theoretical physics framework known as the Theory of Entropicity (ToE). [1, 2] 

## The Core Concept of PoLER
Under the [Theory of Entropicity](https://theory-of-entropicity-toe.pages.dev/ch5-poler-and-second-law), entropy is not viewed merely as a statistical measure of disorder, but rather as the fundamental field of reality from which space, time, and physical laws emerge. PoLER provides a structural replacement for classical mechanics and relativity by asserting that: [1, 3, 4, 5] 

### Entropic Geodesics: 
Physical systems, particles, and bodies follow trajectories that minimize entropic resistance or work.

### Nature of Motion: 
Motion is not a response to forces or fixed spacetime curvature, but rather the relaxation of entropic gradients.

### Emergent Spacetime: 
Spacetime geometry adjusts dynamically to the entropic field. The path a system takes is the one that minimizes cumulative entropic curvature. [2, 5] 

## Overlap with Classical Physics
Rather than contradicting the Second Law of Thermodynamics, PoLER complements it. While the Second Law states that entropy must monotonically increase, **PoLER** dictates the optimal trajectory or path through which that increase occurs. [2] 

The concept builds on the historical Principle of Least Action in classical mechanics. It is part of the **"de Broglie–Haller–Obidi"** lineage of physics, which seeks to prove that classical action is a macro-phenomenon emerging directly from underlying thermodynamic and informational constraints. [2, 6, 7]
