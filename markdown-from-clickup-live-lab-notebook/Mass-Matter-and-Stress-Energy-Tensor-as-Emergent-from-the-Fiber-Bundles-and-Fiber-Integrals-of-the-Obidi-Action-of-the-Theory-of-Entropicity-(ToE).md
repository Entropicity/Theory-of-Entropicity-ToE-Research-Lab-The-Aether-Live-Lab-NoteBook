## Core structure

The theory’s backbone is an **informational manifold**: a geometric object whose points represent informational states rather than ordinary spacetime points, and whose thermodynamic/entropic structure is taken to underlie spacetime, matter, and gravity [8][3]. 

The Theory of Entropicity (ToE) builds an action and field equations on this manifold, then interprets observable physics as a projection or thermodynamic image of that deeper structure [8].

Within that picture, **fiber bundles** supply the mathematical language for attaching a local “internal space” to each point of the base manifold, while **fiber integrals** compute averaged or moment-based quantities over those internal variables [3][10]. In the Theory of Entropicity (ToE), fiber bundles, fiber integrals, statistical manifolds, and moment tensors constitute some of the prerequisites for deriving the theory’s matter-side equations [3].

## How fiber bundles are used

In standard differential geometry, a fiber bundle separates global geometry from local internal data, which makes it suitable for theories where each spacetime or information-space point carries extra structure such as velocity, momentum, phase, or other state variables [10]. ToE uses this setup to treat the informational manifold as the base space and the internal statistical/momentum data as fibers over each point [3].

That is important because the theory wants matter to arise from local entropic distributions rather than from fundamental particles. So the bundle lets Obidi represent a continuum of microstates at each point, while keeping the overall theory geometric and coordinate-independent [3][8]. In other words, the bundle is the scaffold that holds “hidden” entropic content at each point of the manifold.

## How fiber integrals act

A fiber integral collapses the fiber data into effective fields on the base manifold by integrating over the local internal variables [3][10]. In ToE, this is the step where microscopic informational or entropic distributions are turned into macroscopic physical quantities such as density, flux, momentum, or stress–energy [3].

In the Theory of Entropicity (ToE), the **Entropic Stress–Energy Tensor** arises from the **second moment of entropic distributions on momentum fibers** [3]. That means, conceptually and mathematically, that the first moment gives something like bulk flow, while the second moment gives anisotropic pressure and stress terms, which together form a tensor that can play the same role as stress–energy in Einstein-like equations [3]. 

This is the bridge from statistical microstructure to effective matter.

## Mass and matter

Obidi’s framework thus treats **inertial mass** as an entropic or informational effect rather than a primitive property of particles [8]. 

The implication is that when the informational manifold is thermodynamically deformed, the induced effective dynamics on the base look like matter with mass, because the fiber-averaged distributions carry nonzero moments and gradients [8][3].

So “matter” in this scheme is the coarse-grained manifestation of information flow, and “mass” is one of its emergent parameters. 

In the Theory of Entropicity ToE), what we ordinarily call material content is encoded in how entropy is distributed and transported across the fibers, then compressed into tensorial observables by integration [3][8].

## Entropic stress–energy tensor

The **Entropic Stress–Energy Tensor**—also referred to as the **Obidi Stress-Energy Tensor (OSET)**—is presented as the right-hand side of the theory’s field equations [the RHS of Einstein's Field Equations of General Relativity (GR)] [3].

That derivation is structurally analogous to kinetic theory in physics, where a distribution function on phase space yields density, momentum, and stress through moment integrals. ToE repurposes this idea in an informational setting: the distribution is entropic, the fiber variable is momentum-like, and the resulting integrated tensor represents effective energy, momentum, and stress carried by the emergent matter field [3].
