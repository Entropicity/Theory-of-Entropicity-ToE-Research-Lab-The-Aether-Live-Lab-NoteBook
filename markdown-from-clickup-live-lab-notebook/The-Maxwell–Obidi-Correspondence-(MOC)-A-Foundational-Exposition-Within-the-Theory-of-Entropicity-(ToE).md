II.1 Maxwell’s derivation of the electromagnetic wave speed

We begin with Maxwell’s equations in vacuum (no charges, no currents):

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = 0, \qquad \nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0,
$$

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}, \qquad
\nabla \times \mathbf{B} = \mu0 \varepsilon0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t}.
$$

The last equation already includes Maxwell’s displacement current term and is the Ampère–Maxwell law in vacuum.

To obtain the wave equation for the electric field, we take the curl of Faraday’s law:

$$
\nabla \times (\nabla \times \mathbf{E}) = -\frac{\partial}{\partial t} (\nabla \times \mathbf{B}).
$$

Using the standard vector identity

$$
\nabla \times (\nabla \times \mathbf{E}) = \nabla(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E}) - \nabla^2 \mathbf{E},
$$

and the vacuum condition $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = 0$, we obtain

$$
\nabla \times (\nabla \times \mathbf{E}) = -\nabla^2 \mathbf{E}.
$$

On the right-hand side, we substitute the Ampère–Maxwell law:

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{B} = \mu0 \varepsilon0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t},
$$

so that

$$
-\frac{\partial}{\partial t} (\nabla \times \mathbf{B})
= -\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \mu0 \varepsilon0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t} \right)
= -\mu0 \varepsilon0 \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{E}}{\partial t^2}.
$$

Equating both sides gives

$$
-\nabla^2 \mathbf{E} = -\mu0 \varepsilon0 \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{E}}{\partial t^2},
$$

hence

$$
\nabla^2 \mathbf{E} = \mu0 \varepsilon0 \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{E}}{\partial t^2}.
$$

Exactly the same steps applied to $\mathbf{B}$ yield

$$
\nabla^2 \mathbf{B} = \mu0 \varepsilon0 \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{B}}{\partial t^2}.
$$

These are vector wave equations for the electromagnetic field in vacuum.

The universal scalar wave equation in a homogeneous medium is

$$

where $v$ is the wave speed. Comparing this with the electromagnetic wave equation, we identify

Therefore,

$$

This $v$ is the propagation speed of electromagnetic disturbances in vacuum. When numerical values of $\mu0$ and $\varepsilon0$ are inserted, one obtains

$$

Maxwell’s conceptual move was to recognize that the coefficient $\mu0 \varepsilon0$ in his field equations plays the role of $1/v^2$ in the universal wave equation, and to demand that the coefficients match.
