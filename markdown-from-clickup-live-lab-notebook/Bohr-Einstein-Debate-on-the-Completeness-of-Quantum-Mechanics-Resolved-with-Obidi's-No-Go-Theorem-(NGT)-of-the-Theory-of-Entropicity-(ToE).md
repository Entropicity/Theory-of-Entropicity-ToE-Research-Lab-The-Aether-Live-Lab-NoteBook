## 2. The philosophical structure of the Bohr–Einstein debate

### 2.1 Einstein’s position: ontological incompleteness

Einstein’s dissatisfaction with quantum mechanics was not merely emotional or aesthetic; it was rooted in a clear philosophical intuition:
* Quantum mechanics is formally reversible (unitary evolution).  
* The world we inhabit is irreversible (thermodynamic arrow of time, measurement irreversibility, classical macroscopic phenomena).  
* Therefore, quantum mechanics, as it stands, cannot be the final theory; it must be incomplete.

Einstein’s demand was ontological: a complete theory must explain how irreversibility arises and must embed classical reality as a limit, not as an external add‑on.

### 2.2 Bohr’s position: epistemic completeness

Bohr’s reply was fundamentally epistemological:
* Quantum mechanics correctly predicts all observable phenomena.  
* The notion of “incompleteness” is misguided if it demands a description beyond what can be observed and measured.  
* Therefore, quantum mechanics is complete as a theory of phenomena, even if it does not provide a deeper ontological mechanism.

Bohr’s stance is that operational adequacy is sufficient for completeness; Einstein insists that ontological adequacy is required.

The century-long debate is thus a clash between:

* **Epistemic completeness (Bohr):**

completeness = no further predictive refinement.  
* **Ontological completeness (Einstein):**
* completeness = a coherent account of reality, including irreversibility and classicality.

This reframes “completeness” as:

* Not just predictive (Bohr’s criterion).  
* Not just ontological (Einstein’s criterion).

Quantum mechanics, by itself, fails this test. It is reversible, unitary, and silent about the entropic arrow of time. Under NGT, that silence is not neutral; it is a mark of incompleteness.
