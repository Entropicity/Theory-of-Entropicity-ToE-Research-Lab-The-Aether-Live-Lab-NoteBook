# Six Logical Steps on How the Theory of Entropicity (ToE) Derives the Speed of Light (c) as a Structural Universal Maximum Without Resorting to Maxwell's Electro-Magnetic (EM) Constants

In John Onimisi Obidi’s Theory of Entropicity (ToE), the speed of light ($c$) is stripped of its electrical and geometric labels. Rather than postulating its constancy as an unexplainable starting axiom (as Einstein did) or deriving it from electric and magnetic constants (as Maxwell did), ToE derives $c$ as a purely mechanical property of an underlying entropic field. [1, 2, 3] 

The derivation moves through six logical steps to prove that the universal speed limit is the fundamental rate at which the universe dynamically reconfigures its informational state. [4, 5] 

------------------------------
## 1. Formulating the Obidi Action
Instead of tracking electromagnetic waves, ToE tracks small disturbances or ripples moving across a continuous, real scalar field of entropy, $S(x,t)$. By applying fundamental symmetry constraints to this field, a quadratic entropic Lagrangian is uniquely established. When this Lagrangian is mapped over the entropic manifold, it forms the Obidi Action. [4, 5, 6, 7] 

## 2. Emerging the Entropic Wave Equation
When physicists apply the calculus of variations (the Euler-Lagrange equations) to the Obidi Action to find how the field naturally behaves, the resulting math takes the exact form of a classical wave equation. This means that localized changes in entropy do not teleport; they propagate through space as physical "entropic phase waves". [4, 5, 8] 

## 3. The Ratio of Response Coefficients: Stiffness vs. Inertia
In any physical wave equation (like sound traveling through water or a ripple moving down a string), the speed of the wave is determined by a ratio of two intrinsic properties of the medium: restoring force (stiffness) divided by resistance to change (inertia).
ToE identifies two universal parameters built into the fabric of the entropic field: [9] 


### * Entropic Stiffness ($\kappa$): 
The field's inherent tendency to restore its background configuration when disturbed.

### * Entropic Inertia ($\rho_S$): 
The field's physical resistance to sudden changes in entropy flux. [8, 9] 


The characteristic propagation velocity of an entropic wave ($c_{ent}$) is mathematically defined as: [8] 

$$c_{ent} = \sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{\rho_S}}}$$ 


## 4. Planck-Scale Matching (Deriving $\kappa$ and $\rho_S$)
To find what $\kappa$ and $\rho_S$ actually are without guessing, ToE uses dimensional analysis to ground the field in the fundamental units of reality—binding the laws of gravity ($G$), quantum mechanics ($\hbar$), and thermodynamics ($k_B$). From first principles, the independent values are derived as: [8, 9, 10] 

$$\kappa = \frac{k_B c^3}{G} \quad \text{(Entropic Stiffness)}$$ 

$$\rho_S = \frac{k_B c}{G} \quad \text{(Entropic Inertia)}$$ 

When you substitute these expressions back into the wave speed formula, the constants cancel out beautifully: [8] 

$$c_{ent} = \sqrt{\frac{\frac{k_B c^3}{\sqrt{G}}}{\sqrt{\frac{k_B c}{\sqrt{G}}}}} = \sqrt{c^2} = c$$ 

The wave speed of an entropic disturbance is exactly, mathematically equal to the empirically measured speed of light, $c$. [5, 8] 

## 5. Enforcing the No-Rush Theorem
This derivation underpins the No-Rush Theorem (Theorem C.2). Because interactions require a finite duration of entropic synchronization, no physical process, signal, or bit of quantum information can outrun the background field's ability to update itself. This creates a strict causal horizon—the "entropic null cone"—which physically forbids superluminal (faster-than-light) travel. [5, 6, 7, 11] 

## 6. Dynamic Invariance (Why everyone measures the same $c$)
If $c$ is just a background field property, why don't we measure a different speed limit when we run away from or toward a light beam?
ToE explains this through an **Entropic Lorentz Group (ELG).** Clocks, measuring rulers, and human observers are all made of matter, which means they are stable excitations of this same entropic field. 

When you accelerate, your motion through the entropic field encounters "entropic resistance". This resistance physically compresses spatial intervals (length contraction) and slows down internal atomic processes (time dilation). 

Because the tools you use to measure speed are altered in exact proportion to your velocity, the ratio of distance over time always yields precisely the same constant, $c$, in every inertial frame. [1, 7, 10, 11, 12]
